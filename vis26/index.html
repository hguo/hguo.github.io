About this tutorial

Simulations, observatories, and experiments now produce more data than we can store, move, or explore interactively. Lossy compression shrinks scientific data by 10× to 100× — but visualization scientists need to know its impact on visual integrity and analytical correctness.

This tutorial equips visualization researchers and practitioners with the deep working knowledge required to incorporate lossy compression into visualization workflows with confidence. We dissect why scientific floating-point data compresses the way it does, inspect the three foundational stages inside every compressor (decorrelation, quantization, and coding), and compare the design principles of the leading compressors: SZ, ZFP, MGARD, SPERR, and TTHRESH.

Crucially, we tackle the question that matters most to the VIS community: how compression errors propagate through rendering, feature extraction, and analysis pipelines. We demonstrate where standard pointwise compressors distort isosurfaces, volume renderings, streamlines, and topological structures, and introduce state-of-the-art feature-preserving and topology-preserving compressors that provide rigorous guarantees.

The tutorial is presented by the leading researchers in scientific data compression, who have collectively developed the most widely used compressors in this domain (SZ and ZFP, both R&D 100 Award winners). This edition builds on highly rated tutorials given at SC17–25 and ISC17–22, substantially adapted for the visualization audience.

Who should attend

  • Visualization researchers & practitioners who handle massive scientific datasets and need to safeguard visual quality and topological integrity.
  • Application scientists running simulations, experiments, or AI workloads who face critical I/O, transfer, and storage bottlenecks.
  • Graduate students learning about scientific data visualization, data reduction techniques, or designing novel compressors.
  • Infrastructure & tool developers integrating reduction into visualization software, I/O libraries (HDF5, ADIOS), or in situ analysis pipelines.

Prerequisites

A basic understanding of scientific visualization concepts (scalar/vector fields, volume rendering, isosurfaces). No prior expertise in data compression is assumed.

03

Inside a lossy compressor

Almost all modern lossy compressors for numerical scientific data follow a modular three-stage architecture. The techniques selected for each stage determine the compression speed, ratio, and crucially, which visual and topological features survive.

Stage 1

Decorrelation

Reorganizes and conditions the data field via prediction, mathematical transforms, or multi-dimensional decomposition to eliminate spatial and inter-variable redundancy before reduction.

prediction · transforms · tensor decomposition

Stage 2

Reduction

Discards non-critical information under user-defined error bounds (absolute, relative, or feature-based). This is the only lossy stage, and it is where visual distortion and feature artifacts arise.

quantization · filtering · bit truncation

Stage 3

Coding

Losslessly compresses quantized symbols and residual representations by exploiting entropy and repetitive structural patterns to minimize final bitstream size.

run-length · Huffman / arithmetic · embedded

04

What we cover

The tutorial covers six comprehensive topics connecting foundational information theory with cutting-edge visualization and feature-preservation algorithms.

TOPIC 01

Why lossy compression & use cases

Motivation across simulations, physical experiments, and AI workloads. Limits of lossless compression on scientific floating-point data, role of Shannon entropy, smoothness, and redundancy. How lossy compression not only solves storage bottlenecks but cuts I/O times, accelerates in situ visualization, and enables massive scale-up.

TOPIC 02

How lossy compression works

Principles of decorrelation, reduction, and coding stages in detail. Theoretical trade-offs between prediction-based schemes, transform-based methods, and tensor decompositions, and how these mathematical choices propagate into rendering artifacts and analytical distortion.

TOPIC 03

State-of-the-art generic compressors

SZ3, ZFP, MGARD, SPERR, and TTHRESH: design motivations, internal algorithms, throughput benchmarks, and rate–distortion curves. How to harness LibPressio to access 30+ compressors through a single API and use OptZConfig to search optimal parameter spaces under quality constraints.

TOPIC 04 · KEY VIS TOPIC

Impact on visualization & feature preservation

How compression affects volume rendering, isosurface extraction, streamlines, and topological analysis. Why pointwise error bounds fail to preserve scientific features, and how new feature-preserving compressors solve this: TopoSZ for contour trees, MSz for Morse–Smale segmentations, adaptive per-vertex bounds for vector fields, and FFCz for Fourier spectra.

TOPIC 05

Error assessment and trust

Comprehensive error metrics beyond PSNR and SSIM: error autocorrelation, derivative preservation, and physically motivated metrics. Evaluating visual distortion directly rather than raw field residuals. Progressive compression and retrieval for interactive level-of-detail visualization.

TOPIC 06 · FRAMEWORK

Customizing with the FZ framework

Generic compressors are one-size-fits-all and leave significant performance and fidelity on the table. The FZ cyberinfrastructure framework empowers users to synthesize customized compressors for specific scientific domains and visualization pipelines, achieving 2×–17× higher compression ratios with verified feature fidelity.

Software & benchmarks

All compressors and tools demonstrated in the tutorial are open-source and ready for production deployment.

SZ3

Modular, prediction-based error-bounded lossy compressor for floating-point and integer scientific fields.

github.com/szcompressor/SZ3
ZFP

Compressed numerical arrays with high-speed random access; fixed-rate, fixed-precision, and fixed-accuracy modes.

github.com/LLNL/zfp
MGARD

MultiGrid Adaptive Reduction of Data, providing rigorous error bounds on derived scientific quantities of interest.

github.com/CODARcode/MGARD
SPERR

Wavelet-based compressor offering top-tier rate–distortion performance across 2D and 3D scientific fields.

github.com/NCAR/SPERR
TTHRESH

Higher-order tensor decomposition compressor for multidimensional visual and volumetric datasets.

github.com/rballester/tthresh
LibPressio

Universal abstraction interface for 30+ lossless and lossy compressors with bindings in C, C++, Python, and Julia.

github.com/robertu94/libpressio
FZ Framework

Modular cyberinfrastructure framework for synthesizing fine-tunable, specialized lossy compressors.

fzframework.org
Z-checker

Diagnostics suite for assessing compression error: distributions, autocorrelation, derivatives, and spectra.

github.com/CODARcode/Z-checker
QCAT

Quick Compression Analysis Toolkit for rapidly computing rate-distortion, PSNR, and SSIM metrics.

github.com/szcompressor/qcat
SDRBench

Standard scientific data reduction benchmark datasets from climate, cosmology, fusion, and combustion.

sdrbench.github.io
